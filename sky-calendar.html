    How this calendar works

    Every date and time on this page is calculated in your own browser with the open-source Astronomy Engine library. Nothing is sent to a server. For the location you choose, it finds the four Moon phases (including Amavasya and Purnima), equinoxes and solstices, lunar and solar eclipses, oppositions of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, and the greatest elongations of Mercury and Venus. It also works out sunrise, sunset, astronomical twilight and moonrise for any day you click. Tonight's plan combines these to find the darkest stretch of the night and the planets that are well above the horizon.

    Light pollution & the Bortle scale

    The Bortle scale rates how dark a sky is, from 1 (a pristine desert or mountain sky) to 9 (an inner-city sky). Most Indian city centres sit around Bortle 8, where only the Moon, planets and the brightest stars show. Move to a village or a remote hill site and the Milky Way appears. Choose your surroundings above, or enter your own Bortle class, and the planner will only promise what your sky can really show. Learn how to read your sky in the Stargazer's Field Guide.

    Nakshatras & Sankranti

    Indian calendars track the Moon through 27 Nakshatras of 13°20' each, and mark a Sankranti whenever the Sun moves into a new sidereal rāśi (sign). This page measures longitudes against the stars using an approximate Lahiri ayanamsa, so the Nakshatra and Sankranti dates match traditional panchangs to within about a day. Makar Sankranti, when the Sun enters Makara in mid-January, is the best known. These are cultural and calendrical markers. The page makes no astrological predictions.

    Times are calculated in your browser with Astronomy Engine (accuracy ≈ 1 arc-minute) and shown in your device's time zone. Meteor shower peaks are approximate (IMO).
