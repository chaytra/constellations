Indigenous Sky Lore & Heritage

The Sky Through Indian Eyes: Nakshatras & Saptarishi

Long before the Greek heroic myths became standard across modern international star maps, Indian astronomers in the Vedic era had mapped the heavens with mathematical precision into 27 lunar mansions (Nakshatras).

By Chaitra · IIIT Allahabad · Updated 13 November 2026

The Architecture of the 27 Nakshatras

While the Sun appears to complete one circuit through the zodiac constellations over 365 days, the Moon does it every 27.3 days (the sidereal lunar month). To track the Moon's daily progress, Indian astronomers divided the 360-degree celestial ecliptic into 27 equal segments of 13°20' each.

Each segment is anchored by a prominent star or asterism known as the Yogatara (junction star). When an Indian calendar refers to a day's Nakshatra, it identifies which celestial palace the Moon is visiting on that night.

Three Great Starlore Traditions

1. Saptarishi (The Seven Sages) & Arundhati-Vasishtha

The stars of the Big Dipper represent the Saptarishi: Kratu, Pulaha, Pulastya, Atri, Angiras, Vasishtha, and Marichi. In traditional Indian weddings, the bride and groom were invited to step outside at dusk to view Vasishtha and Arundhati (the visual binary stars Mizar and Alcor in the Dipper's handle).

Unlike other multiple star systems where a smaller star orbits a dominant primary, Mizar and Alcor appear to revolve in serene equilibrium alongside one another—a poetic emblem of egalitarian companionship in marriage, as well as a sharp historical test of eyesight!

2. Dhruva (Polaris): The Unshakeable Prince

In Indian mythology, young prince Dhruva performed profound penance to Lord Vishnu seeking a place of unshakeable honor that no one could take away. Pleased with his devotion, Vishnu placed him as Dhruva Nakshatra (Polaris), the steadfast pivot around which all the planets, stars, and even the seven mighty sages must orbit.

3. Mrigavyadha & Ardra: The Cosmic Hunter and the Deer

In the Rigveda, the constellation Orion was viewed as a celestial stag or deer (Mriga), with the delicate head stars representing Mrigashira. Sirius (the brightest star in Canis Major) is Mrigavyadha or Lubdhaka, the fierce archer who aims his arrow across the celestial river at the deer. Red Betelgeuse is Ardra, representing teardrops and the intense transformative energy of Rudra (Shiva).
