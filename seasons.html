Constellation Atlas

The Sky by Season: A Year-Round Tour

Because Earth orbits the Sun, the night side of our planet points towards different depths of the cosmos through the seasons. Use the interactive filters below to discover what shines brightest right now.

By Chaitra · IIIT Allahabad · Updated 13 November 2026

Filter Constellations by Season

Select a season to filter visible constellations. All landmarks listed are chosen for prime evening visibility from northern latitudes, including all of India.

  • Winter

    Orion (The Hunter)

    Look for three bright stars in a short, straight row: Orion's belt. Reddish Betelgeuse marks the right shoulder, and blue-white Rigel shines at the foot. Just below the belt hangs the Orion Nebula (M42), a radiant stellar nursery visible to the unaided eye.

    Indian Astronomy: Orion's head stars form Mrigashira, and Betelgeuse is the stormy star Ardra (associated with Rudra).

  • Winter

    Taurus (The Bull)

    Extend Orion's belt up and to the right to reach fierce orange Aldebaran, the eye of the bull. A short hop further brings you to the Pleiades (Seven Sisters), a breathtaking open cluster that resembles a miniature dipper.

    Indian Astronomy: The Pleiades represent Krittika, and Aldebaran is Rohini, two prominent Nakshatras.

  • Winter

    Canis Major (The Greater Dog)

    Follow Orion's belt downwards and to the left to reach Sirius, the brightest individual star in the entire night sky. Shimmering in iridescent flashes through atmospheric refraction, it commands the winter horizon.

    Indian Astronomy: Sirius is celebrated as Mrigavyadha (the deer hunter) or Lubdhaka.

  • Winter

    Gemini (The Twins)

    Hovering northeast of Orion are the twin stars Castor and Pollux. Pollux shines with a warm golden hue, while Castor is a complex six-star multiple system visible as a binary through modest telescopes.

    Indian Astronomy: Known as Punarvasu, symbolizing renewal and return of light.

  • Spring

    Ursa Major (The Great Bear)

    The iconic Big Dipper rides high overhead on spring evenings. Follow the outer bowl pointer stars to locate Polaris. Look closely at Mizar in the handle to test your eyesight on its companion star Alcor.

    Indian Astronomy: Revered as the Saptarishi (Seven Sages); Mizar and Alcor are Vasishtha and Arundhati.

  • Spring

    Leo (The Lion)

    Recognizable by the "Sickle" asterism—a backward question mark crowned by brilliant blue-white Regulus. A graceful triangle of stars to the east outlines the lion's body and tail (Denebola).

    Indian Astronomy: Regulus represents Magha ("the mighty one"), associated with ancestral wisdom.

  • Spring

    Boötes (The Herdsman)

    Follow the graceful arc of the Big Dipper's handle southwards to spot Arcturus, a blazing orange-red giant star and the fourth-brightest star in the sky.

    Indian Astronomy: Arcturus corresponds to Swati, celebrated for its solitary radiance.

  • Summer

    Scorpius (The Scorpion)

    One of the few constellations that genuinely looks like its namesake. Featuring a long curved stinger tail and ruby-red supergiant Antares (the "heart of the scorpion") hanging low on the southern horizon from India.

    Indian Astronomy: Antares is known as Jyeshtha ("the senior or eldest").

  • Summer

    Cygnus & The Summer Triangle

    Cygnus (The Swan) flies along the Milky Way with blue-white Deneb at its tail. Together with Vega (in Lyra) and Altair (in Aquila), these three luminous stars dominate high overhead throughout monsoon and summer nights.

    Indian Astronomy: Vega is Abhijit and Altair is Shravana, integral to traditional calendar reckoning.

  • Summer

    Sagittarius (The Archer)

    Prominently shaped like a "Teapot", Sagittarius points straight towards the supermassive black hole at the center of our Milky Way galaxy, surrounded by rich star fields and nebulae.

    Indian Astronomy: Encompasses the Nakshatras Mula and Purva Ashadha.

  • Autumn

    Cassiopeia (The Queen)

    A striking zigzag "W" or "M" formation of five bright stars wheeling opposite the Big Dipper. It provides an infallible navigational aid when the Dipper is too low to see.

    Indian Astronomy: Traditionally viewed as Sharmishtha in classical starlore.

  • Autumn

    Pegasus & Andromeda

    The prominent Great Square of Pegasus spans autumn skies. Hopping off the northeastern corner leads to Andromeda and M31—the Andromeda Galaxy, visible as a gentle oval mist 2.5 million light-years away.

    Indian Astronomy: Star pairs in Pegasus correspond to Purva Bhadrapada and Uttara Bhadrapada.

How the Celestial Clockwork Works

Because Earth completes a 360-degree orbit around the Sun every 365.25 days, the stars rise approximately 4 minutes earlier each night. Over 30 days, that translates to a two-hour shift in the sky.

Observer's Tip: If you spot a constellation at 10:00 PM in November, it will occupy roughly that same position at 8:00 PM in December, and at 6:00 PM in January!
