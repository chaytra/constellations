Celestial Temples of India: Where Architecture Meets the Sky

Many Indian temples are built around the sky: Sun temples that catch the equinox sunrise, and a circuit of nine shrines for the nine grahas.

By Chaitra · IIIT Allahabad · Updated 18 November 2026

Indian temple architecture often records the Sun's yearly path in stone. Some sanctums face the point where the Sun rises at the equinoxes, and carved wheels or pillars track its shadow and light through the seasons. These alignments show how carefully ancient builders watched the sky.

The Navagraha (“nine grahas”) tradition links nine celestial influences to deities: the Sun, the Moon, the five naked-eye planets (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn), and Rahu and Ketu. A graha is not the same as a planet in modern astronomy. The Sun is a star, the Moon is Earth's satellite, and Rahu and Ketu are not bodies at all. They are the two lunar nodes, the points where the Moon's tilted orbit crosses the Sun's path, and they are exactly where eclipses happen. That is the bridge between this tradition and the astronomy on the rest of this site. The temples below are described by what they are traditionally dedicated to; this page makes no astrological claims.

The temples

Showing all 15 sites. The first nine form the Navagraha circuit around Kumbakonam (Tamil Nadu) and Karaikal (Puducherry).

  • Surya → the Sun

    ☀️ Suryanar Kovil

    Near Kumbakonam, Thanjavur district, Tamil Nadu

    Dedicated to Surya, this Chola-era temple (traditionally dated to the reign of Kulottunga I, around the 11th–12th century) is the hub of the Navagraha circuit. Unusually, it has shrines for all the other eight grahas around the main Surya shrine.

    Sky link: Watch where the Sun rises on your horizon through the year: it rises due east only at the equinoxes and swings north and south towards the solstices.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Chandra → the Moon

    🌙 Kailasanathar Temple

    Thingalur, near Thiruvaiyaru, Tamil Nadu

    A Shiva temple where Chandra, the Moon, is worshipped as one of the Navagrahas. Moonlight is said to fall on the linga on certain full-Moon nights of the Tamil months of Panguni and Purattasi.

    Sky link: Follow the Moon's phases from Amavasya (new Moon) to Purnima (full Moon): one full cycle takes about 29.5 days.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Angaraka / Mangala → Mars

    🔴 Vaitheeswaran Koil

    Mayiladuthurai district, Tamil Nadu

    Shiva is worshipped here as Vaidyanatha, the divine healer, and the temple holds the Navagraha shrine of Angaraka (Mars). It is one of the most visited temples of the circuit.

    Sky link: Mars looks like a steady orange-red ‘star’. It is brightest around opposition, roughly every 26 months.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Budha → Mercury

    ☿️ Swetharanyeswarar Temple

    Thiruvenkadu, near Poompuhar, Tamil Nadu

    A large Shiva temple near the Kaveri delta coast whose separate shrine to Budha makes it the Mercury temple of the Navagraha circuit.

    Sky link: Mercury never strays far from the Sun. Look low in the twilight near its greatest elongations, shortly after sunset or before sunrise.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Guru / Brihaspati → Jupiter

    🟡 Apatsahayesvarar Temple

    Alangudi, Thiruvarur district, Tamil Nadu

    Here Guru (Jupiter) is worshipped in the form of Dakshinamurthy, Shiva as the teacher. Thursdays (Guruvar) traditionally draw the largest crowds.

    Sky link: Jupiter is usually the brightest ‘star’ of the night after Venus. Binoculars show up to four of its moons.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Shukra → Venus

    ⚪ Agneeswarar Temple

    Kanjanur, near Kumbakonam, Tamil Nadu

    Unlike most Navagraha temples, there is no separate idol of Shukra here. Shiva himself is venerated in the role of Shukra (Venus).

    Sky link: Venus is the brilliant ‘evening star’ or ‘morning star’ and is easiest to see around its greatest elongations.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Shani → Saturn

    🪐 Dharbaranyeswarar Temple

    Thirunallar, Karaikal, Puducherry

    The temple of Shani (Saturn) draws very large crowds for Shani Peyarchi, the day Saturn moves into a new rāśi. Saturn's slow 29.5-year orbit means this happens only about every two and a half years.

    Sky link: Saturn looks like a steady pale-yellow star. Even a small telescope shows its rings.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Rahu → the Moon's ascending node

    🌘 Naganathaswamy Temple

    Thirunageswaram, near Kumbakonam, Tamil Nadu

    Rahu, the serpent graha, has his shrine here. Rahu is not a physical body. It is the point where the Moon's orbit crosses the Sun's path going north, and eclipses can happen only when the Sun and Moon are near such a node.

    Sky link: Eclipses come in ‘seasons’ about six months apart, when the Sun lines up with the lunar nodes. Check the Sky Calendar for the next one.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Ketu → the Moon's descending node

    🌒 Naganathaswamy Temple

    Keezhaperumpallam, near Poompuhar, Tamil Nadu

    The Ketu temple of the circuit. Ketu is Rahu's partner: the opposite crossing point of the Moon's orbit, where it moves south across the ecliptic. Together, Rahu and Ketu are the traditional explanation for eclipses.

    Sky link: A lunar eclipse happens at full Moon near a node, and a solar eclipse at new Moon near a node.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Surya → the Sun

    🛞 Sun Temple, Konark

    Puri district (13th century, UNESCO World Heritage Site), Odisha

    Built around 1250 CE under King Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, the temple is designed as Surya's colossal chariot, with 24 carved stone wheels drawn by seven horses. The wheels' spokes are widely described as working sundials that let you read the time from their shadows.

    Sky link: The temple faces east, towards the rising Sun. Notice how shadows shorten towards noon and lengthen again in the afternoon.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Surya → the Sun

    🌅 Sun Temple, Modhera

    Mehsana district (1026–27 CE), Gujarat

    Built under King Bhima I of the Chaulukya dynasty, this temple is aligned so that the first rays of the rising Sun on the equinoxes reach the sanctum. The original image of Surya they once lit is now missing. In front lies the stepped tank Surya Kund.

    Sky link: On the March and September equinoxes the Sun rises due east and day and night are nearly equal.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Surya → the Sun (Makar Sankranti)

    🕳️ Gavi Gangadhareshwara Temple

    Gavipuram, Bengaluru, Karnataka

    A cave temple carved into a monolithic rock. Every year around Makar Sankranti (mid-January), evening sunlight passes between the horns of the Nandi statue and falls on the linga inside for a few minutes, drawing large crowds when the sky is clear.

    Sky link: Around Makar Sankranti the Sun is entering sidereal Makara (Capricorn) and the days in India are starting to lengthen.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Surya → the Sun's path through the 12 rāśis

    🏛️ Vidyashankara Temple

    Sringeri, Chikkamagaluru district (14th century), Karnataka

    The hall of this Vijayanagara-era temple has 12 pillars carved with the signs of the zodiac. Sunlight is said to fall on the pillar of the rāśi the Sun currently occupies. The alignment has been studied by N. Kameswara Rao and Priya Thakur (Journal of Astronomical History and Heritage, 2011).

    Sky link: Each Sankranti, when the Sun moves into a new rāśi, is marked on the Sky Calendar.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • The prime meridian of classical Indian astronomy

    📐 Mahakaleshwar & the Vedh Shala

    Ujjain, Madhya Pradesh

    Home to Mahakaleshwar, one of the twelve Jyotirlingas, Ujjain served as the zero meridian of longitude for classical Indian astronomers. Maharaja Jai Singh II built an observatory here (the Vedh Shala, or Jantar Mantar) in the early 18th century. The Tropic of Cancer runs just north of the city.

    Sky link: Near the Tropic of Cancer the noon Sun stands almost directly overhead around the June solstice.

    Open in Google Maps ↗

  • Brihaspati (Jupiter) & Surya (Sun)

    🌊 Triveni Sangam

    Prayagraj, Uttar Pradesh

    At the confluence of the Ganga, Yamuna and the mythical Saraswati, the timing of the Kumbh Mela is traditionally set by the positions of Jupiter and the Sun, with the Sun in Makara (Capricorn). The annual Magh Mela is also held while the Sun is in Makara. Readers at IIIT Allahabad can observe from the Sangam ghats, away from the city lights.

    Sky link: Jupiter takes about 12 years to circle the zodiac, which is why a full Kumbh at Prayagraj returns roughly every 12 years.

    Open in Google Maps ↗
