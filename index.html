Constellations for beginners: how to find your way around the night sky

The same stars that guided sailors, farmers and storytellers for thousands of years are still overhead tonight. All you need is a clear evening, a little patience and a few easy tricks to recognise them.

What is a constellation, really?

A constellation is a patch of the sky that people have grouped together and named, often after a hero, an animal or an object. Today the International Astronomical Union recognises 88 official constellations, which together cover the whole sky like countries on a map.

Two things surprise most beginners. First, the stars in a constellation are usually not close to each other. They only line up from our point of view, and some are hundreds of light-years closer to us than their neighbours. Second, a famous pattern is not always a constellation. The Big Dipper is an asterism, a recognisable shape that is part of the larger constellation Ursa Major.

The stories change with the culture. The seven bright stars that many people call the Plough or the Big Dipper are the Saptarishi, the seven sages, in Indian tradition. Same stars, different story.

Before you go out

  • Give your eyes time. Night vision takes about 20 to 30 minutes to develop fully. A single glance at a bright phone screen resets much of it.
  • Use a red light. A torch covered in red cellophane, or a phone in a dark red mode, protects your night vision while you read a chart.
  • Escape the glare. Move away from street lights and bright windows. A rooftop or open ground, even a short distance from town, shows far more stars.
  • Check the Moon. A bright Moon washes out faint stars. The nights around the new Moon are best for deep, dark skies.
  • Binoculars beat nothing, and nothing beats patience. You do not need a telescope. An ordinary pair of binoculars reveals star clusters and the Moon's craters.

Find north first

Everything else is easier once you know where north is. The trick is Polaris, the North Star, which sits almost exactly above Earth's north pole, so it barely moves while the rest of the sky turns around it. In India it is known as Dhruva, the steady one. It is not especially bright, but it is easy to find:

  1. Find the Big Dipper, seven stars shaped like a ladle or a plough.
  2. Take the two stars on the outer edge of the bowl, Merak and Dubhe.
  3. Draw a line through them, away from the bowl, and follow it for about five times the gap between them.
  4. The moderately bright star you land on is Polaris. Face it and you are looking north.

A useful fact: the height of Polaris above the horizon roughly equals your latitude. From northern India, around 25° north, it sits low, about a fist and a half above the northern horizon.

The sky by season

Because Earth orbits the Sun, the part of the sky that faces us after dark slowly changes through the year. These are the evening highlights for the northern hemisphere, so they work well from India. Use the buttons to filter by season.

  • Winter

    Orion

    Look for three bright stars in a short, straight row: Orion's belt. Reddish Betelgeuse and blue-white Rigel mark a shoulder and a foot. The fuzzy glow below the belt is the Orion Nebula, a stellar nursery you can spot without a telescope.

    In Indian tradition, the stars of Orion's head form Mrigashira, and Betelgeuse is Ardra.

  • Winter

    Taurus

    Follow the belt up and to the right to orange Aldebaran, the glowing eye of the bull. Just beyond is the Pleiades, a tiny cluster that most people see as six or seven soft stars.

    The Pleiades are Krittika and Aldebaran is Rohini, both among the 27 nakshatras, the traditional lunar mansions.

  • Winter

    Canis Major

    Follow the belt down and to the left to Sirius, the brightest star in the whole night sky. It often twinkles in flashes of colour when it is low.

    Sirius is Mrigavyadha, the hunter who aims at the deer.

  • Spring

    Ursa Major

    The Big Dipper rides high on spring evenings. Look closely at the middle star of the handle, Mizar. A fainter star beside it, Alcor, has long been used as an eyesight test.

    The seven stars are the Saptarishi. Mizar and Alcor are the sage Vasishtha and his wife Arundhati.

  • Spring

    Leo

    Find a backwards question mark, the "Sickle", with bright Regulus at its base. A triangle of stars to the east forms the lion's hindquarters.

    Regulus corresponds to Magha, another of the nakshatras.

  • Summer

    Scorpius

    A curved tail with a hooked stinger, and red Antares at its heart. From northern India it hangs low in the south, so you need a clear horizon.

    Antares is Jyeshtha, "the eldest".

  • Summer

    Cygnus and the Summer Triangle

    Cygnus is a great cross flying along the Milky Way, with Deneb at its tail. Deneb, Vega and Altair together form the Summer Triangle, an easy shape to spot overhead.

    Vega is Abhijit and Altair is Shravana.

  • Autumn

    Cassiopeia and Andromeda

    Cassiopeia is a bold "W" (or "M") high in the north. From the Great Square of Pegasus, hop along Andromeda to a faint smudge: the Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away and one of the most distant things you can see with your own eyes.

Cassiopeia and the Big Dipper circle Polaris on opposite sides, like two children on a seesaw. When one is low, the other is high, so on almost any clear night one of them can help you find north.

The sky through Indian eyes

Indian astronomy divides the Moon's path into 27 nakshatras, each tied to a star or small group of stars. Many familiar western names have matching Indian ones. Traditions and spellings vary by region and text, so treat this as a friendly guide rather than a rulebook.

Four star-hopping tricks

  1. Belt to Sirius. Extend Orion's belt down and to the left to reach the brightest star in the sky.
  2. Belt to the Pleiades. Extend the belt the other way, up and to the right, through Aldebaran and on to the Pleiades.
  3. Dipper to Polaris. Use the two pointer stars as shown above.
  4. Dipper's handle to Spica. In spring, follow the curve of the handle ("arc to Arcturus") then speed on to Spica, a bright blue-white star in Virgo.

Free tools make practice easier. Stellarium Web shows a live sky chart for your location, and the NASA Night Sky Network lists stargazing events and guides.

Questions beginners ask

Why do constellations change with the seasons?

Earth orbits the Sun, so the direction our night side faces slowly shifts. In winter evenings we look out toward Orion; in summer evenings, toward Scorpius and the centre of the Milky Way.

Are the stars in a constellation close together?

Usually not. They just line up in our view. One star in a pattern may be tens or hundreds of light-years farther away than its neighbour.

Why do stars twinkle but planets usually don't?

Starlight is a single point that Earth's moving air bends and flickers. A planet appears as a tiny disc, so the flickers average out and it shines steadier.

Which star is the brightest?

Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky. Planets such as Venus and Jupiter can outshine it, but they are not stars.

Do I need a telescope?

No. Every constellation on this page is visible with the naked eye. Binoculars are a cheap, rewarding upgrade.
